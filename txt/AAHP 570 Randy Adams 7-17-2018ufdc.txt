Interviewee: Randy Adams Interviewer: Marie Kroeger Date: July 17, 2018



K:  This is Marie Kroeger. I am a volunteer for the Matheson History Museum and the Samuel Proctor Oral History Program at the University of Florida. Today’s date is July 17, 2018. I am interviewing Randy Adams at the Matheson History Museum Library Archives building in Gainesville, Florida. So, Randy could you tell us your full name?

	A:	My full name is Randy Sanford Adams. S-A-N-F-O-R-D. Adams, A-D-A-M-S. K:	Thank you for spelling the middle name.

	A:	Randy is not short for Randal or Randolph; it is Randy. R-A-N-D-Y. K:	And the middle name was—

	A:	Sanford. S-A-N-F-O-R-D.



	K:	Okay, very good. Where were you born? A:	Shellman, Georgia.

	K:	The town name?



	A:	Shellman. S-H-E-L-L-M-A-N.



	K:	Okay, very good. And when was that? A:	January 14, 1955.

	K:	We got that taken care of now. Basics, vitals, whatever. Were you raised there?



	A:	No ma’am, my parents moved down to Cocoa, Florida when I was two years old, so in 1957 they relocated down to Cocoa, Florida where I grew up there.





	K:	So you’ve been there for some time?



	A:	Yes ma’am, I lived there, went through high school there, went through junior college there. Then I left in 1975; came up to live in Gainesville while I attended the University of Florida for two years.

	K:	Do you have any brothers or sisters?



	A:	I have one brother who’s living, Vernon Adams; he is a ward of the state. He don’t want to do right, so he is doing a lot of time in prison. The rest of my family is deceased. I had two brothers. Oldest brother was Larry Adams. He is a Vietnam veteran. And Leonard Adams the ones above me, they are both Vietnam veterans. They both came back from Vietnam, but Agent Orange killed Larry in 2001, and it killed Leonard in 2014 and I am the third child. My sister’s name was Terry Adams; she died 2011, so basically I’m it. My father died in 1989 and my mother died in 2007, so I’m about the last of seven.

	K:	Yeah, that is sort of sad.



	A:	It is tough, but through the grace of God, I stand.



	K:	So, you grew up, brothers and sisters, in Cocoa, Florida A:	Yep.

	K:	Your parents worked? Father worked?



	A:	My dad worked for Pan American, but it was out at Kennedy Space Center from 1957 through 1989. He died in [19]89. My mom worked there. She retired from the Brevard County Health Department. She died 2007.





	K:	So everybody was busy. So you would have started school in the early [19]60s?



	A:	Yes ma’am, I started 1961. I started school when I was six and a half, ‘cause back then my mom told the state of Florida wouldn’t let you start when you were five. So I was born in January in the middle of the year so I was already tall for my age, so I kind of sort of had a complex as I was always taller than everyone, but they made me start when I was six and a half. Yeah so I started in 1961.

	K:	Was that school integrated at that time?



	A:	No ma’am. Funny thing, I’m glad you asked that because from 1961 through 1964, that’s first through fourth grade, I went to Poinsette Elementary P-O-I-N-S- E-T-T-E Elementary School in Cocoa, Florida. I didn’t know this at the time, but I found out later as I got in junior high school apparently they passed a law in [19]64 to integrate schools, but Florida rejected it and they did not integrate schools until 1965. So in 1965 they took about fifty-five of us Black students from out where we were living out in West Cocoa and they sent us to the integrated school called Golfview Elementary that was in Rockledge, Florida, 1965; beginning of the school year, I think was September. And it scared me because I saw it on TV but I never thought it would happen to me. So I was the oldest of my brothers; my younger brother and sister; I was in the fifth grade. If I remember there was police officers and it was, I still remember, it was like a gauntlet. We had to walk through two lines of people yelling and screaming at us; calling us racist names and telling us to go home and spitting on us. That shocked me, and I got through it and it made me stronger. Before you know it, I started excelling; the kids didn’t accept us but it’s been integrated ever since 1965.





	K:	How long did that difficult period go on?



	A:	I would say for the life of – for the fifth and sixth grade there were pockets of it, you know. But some kids, you know – kids are kids and we’d have things in common, so I was good at sports, I was good at drawing, I was good in math; I was good in all the subjects. I wrote poetry, I did art, I was a practical joker and I was on the patrol – keeping order and stuff. It was there, and I learned how to deal with it and the ones who were going to be jerks, were always jerks. To credit, I forget some of them, I believe Robin Pickett was one, he was . . . There were always people who – I hate to say it, but there were always White folks who would stand up and chastise those people who were being ugly toward us. So it’s good and bad everywhere. But I have fond memories of my teacher Miss Mitchell was one of them; she encouraged me. Miss Newson, Mr. Dean; I’ll never forget them. They encouraged me to go farther and not just stagnate but stretch myself.

	K:	Yeah, were the teachers integrated or did you have only White teachers?



	A:	Let me see, in fifth grade, I think, no, but in the sixth grade I think there was maybe one or two cause remember that’s in the infancy of this, I call it “The Grand Experiment.” By the time I got to junior high school it was more integrated. Teachers were phased out; they saw that all traditional Black schools by then in 1967 through [19]68 and that’s when I really started having problems, cause there was a lot of fights, stuff like that and I couldn’t see – not trying to be utopian, but even as a young kid, I just trying to see why this is like this, why are they like this, why would someone hate me. I just couldn’t see it. But, I still somehow rose through it all. Stayed on my art; stayed on my poetry. Had some





good friends, I got a lifelong friend Bruce; I met in 1969 when I was in math reading cause they had some problems but him and I are like brothers – he played basketball; I been friends ever since; Jim Beal been friends ever since.

	K:	That’s handy now.



	A:	Yeah, and we are like brothers, so I say that on all sides of all races, people who have bad hearts are going to have bad hearts; bigotry, racism, whatever is not systemic of any races – everyone, if you are bad, you are just bad. My lifelong friends are not Black or White guys I don’t see that in them and they don’t see that in me. I just say, “Hey Bruce”.

	K:	You have just answered a few of the questions I was going to ask you; which is good. No, that’s good. I was going to ask about sports and you were active in sports.

	A:	Yeah, for a while in high school I ran track, I played football, then I played basketball and the reason I stayed with basketball [laughter] I noticed, it’s kind of funny. When I played football, we sat on a bench, we all get all muddy and grubby and the girls that had the fence that separated us, so we got on the bus is where we went to the locker rooms. The girls that watch basketball is more intimate, we play right down on the court and after we win, the girls come and stole our basketball, which Theresa, my girlfriend who is now my wife; she used to complain about; I can’t get to you because of all the cheer leaders are there. I met her when I was in the tenth grade; she was in the ninth grade and we’ve been together ever since.





	K:	My goodness that is a story in itself. Did you do anything with music, or did they have any?

	A:	They had music; I probably should have because I love music, and I try to play the piano [inaudible 10:12] Mary Alice. I grew up in the rural area of Cocoa. I was always in the swamps; I was always hunting and fishing, catching snakes. All my spare time, I was either growing something in the garden, doing my art work, writing poetry, or cutting school to be out in the woods to go fishing or something. I just had too many other things to do besides music.

	K:	Yes, well you weren’t wasting your time.



	A:	I’m still kind of like that I need to be five people right now; I got so many interests. K:	So now looking back, how do you feel about your public school education?

	A:	I have I made some lasting relationships, I had good teachers that gave me examples, one them Bruce [inaudible 11:06] his father was a coach in the ninth grade, a PE coach and I’d always go to their house and talk to me and treat me like a person. You learn things from people that have good leadership skills and good family skills. My French teacher Mr. - I forget his name now – he was a Black fellow, he taught me how to live – people realize you go to school to get an education really, to pass tests or whatever you learn the basics, but I didn’t see that and it inspired me to go on to college to be a school teacher. Because teachers should, and some of mine did, inspire you to be a person of society and they should have good examples and this is some bad examples, but I learned a





lot. You know I admired them and we hung out a lot together – I just stuck around them. I learned a lot of good things from them.

	K:	That’s good.



	A:	And how it made me feel, I wanted to make somebody feel, so I turned down all my basketball scholarships, which made my momma mad at me, and I wanted to concentrate on being an art education teacher and that’s what I graduated with from the University of Florida.

	K:	That’s a story one doesn’t hear very often.



	A:	I had a lot of offers; I just didn’t want to – they were going to limit me to nine credit hours a semester to play basketball. If you do the math, I never would have graduated. And back in [19]73 through [19]77 I was told by one of my basketball coaches in high school, “Randy if you are not at least six feet 7 inches (I am only 6 five and ½) no matter how high you can jump or how high [inaudible 12:46] almost touch the top of the back board, no matter how high you can jump, the pros will never look at you.”

	K:	[interruption - cut 12:53 to 13:03]



	A:	I just decided God bless a lot of folks I realized if I do four years of college and I graduate and I can’t go into the pros why would I play basketball. So I turned them down and through the grace of God, I also had a enough grade point average in high school and junior college I got a full ride academic scholarship at the University of Florida.





	K:	That’s very good!



	A:	I just went to school. At my highest time, I took twenty-one semester hours per quarter.

	K:	Wow! Talk about being busy.



	A:	But it worked out; in my senior year I only had one or two classes a semester, so I cooled out pretty good. [Laughter] So I it worked out - I learned a lot from my teachers and I still admire a lot of them, I still remember a lot of the names.

	K:	So you graduated from high school in Cocoa A:	In 1973.

	K:	In [19]73 and then you went to community college.



	A:	Yes ma’am, Brevard Community College there in Cocoa, Florida – 1973 to 1975. K:	And then you came up to the University of Florida.

	A:	Yes, ma’am, [19]75 through [19]77. K:	And majored in . . .

	A:	Art Education; minored in English. K:	Sounds very solid.

	A:	Not to brag about, I was a 3.7 I think but I graduated with honors and I think I was



3.25 or something – I kind of backed off my senior year. So a couple of people I never met when I got in the military, once I got commissioned, a lot of officers would say, “You enlisted people are so egotistical.” One of them asked me – guy





named, well I won’t say his named, a junior officer, he asked me, “What is your degree in?” I said Art Education; he said “It’s not very competitive.” I said, “What’s yours in?” He said “Soil Engineering”. So I said, “You are a glorified sod buster.” He said, “What do you mean?” I said, “Don’t look at yourself as being better than these people – these enlisted people, don’t look down on them”. I said, because as you look down on me, I said if you remember college, our first two years we took the same courses and the second you go off into your major and I’m not a dummy cause I took Art Education, I choose to, but I had a 3.7 average in the same courses you took. Don’t look down on people cause he had a problem with being a new officer and never been enlisted; he was talking down to the enlisted troops in the military – you can’t do that because that is your back bone – treat them like men. They will respect you not because of your rank, [inaudible 15:48]

	K:	So then you graduated from college and you went into the military.



	A:	I tried to get a job for a while. I went into the military – I graduated college in May or June 1977; asked my wife to marry me and we got married in March 1978.

Couldn’t find a job so I decided, well, I am going to go join the Army that [inaudible 16:21] twice join the Army. Nah, I just don’t like – I didn’t have a good feel for it, then the Marines tried to get me, and I didn’t want to do that, so I went and asked the Navy recruiter; I said, “Would you make me a E-4 like the Army promised me, cause I have college?” He said, “Yes” and I said, “Sign me up”. So I went in the Navy; 1978, March 3.





	K:	Okay. How did your brothers and sisters – your brothers particularly I guess, or your family, feel about that?

	A:	My mom was scared to death. There was no war; it was post Viet Nam but I still have my draft card, but I think Viet Nam stopped in [19]76, I believe, but she was scared to death because – Mom loved me to death; she loved all of us, but she wanted to hang on me too much. She didn’t want me to get married either but I just wanted to get married. My wife and I had been going together for seven years and I wanted to get on with my life; I want to have a family. I wanted to have the means to take care of, so I joined the military, and they offer good training, good opportunities, I became an electrician and there was good money when you get out. I just saw the future, and the main reason I joined the military; I’ll never forget, I was a senior in college getting ready to graduate and I remember seeing on the TV in my room in my dorm room, that there were students at Yale, Harvard, and another Ivy League school – protesting the military, protesting the United States. Back then I was a typical hippy protesting: “Yada yada” everything cause it was cool, but something hit me, I said, “What are you all protesting? You are living in the lap of luxury; you are in a Ivy League school – you got all that money- you come from big family with money; what are you protesting?” Something hit me in the back of my head, Marie, it said, Randy, what are you protesting; you went to school on a scholarship somebody paid your way, and you running your mouth and you haven’t paid no dues. So I shocked people after I graduated, I cut my hair; had a huge afro, I cut my hair down and I decided in 1977, I’m going to join the Navy. I’m gonna see what this





is all about and I’m going to travel and see what I’m talking about before I protest. And what I found, Marie, is that this country, even I said now, may have its faults, and there are many, but still all in all, we are the greatest country in the world.

It’s okay to complain then do something about it, but we need to count our blessings also. So I am not right wing, I’m not left wing; I’m just a Christian man I look at all sides. There is good in everybody but where there is problems we need to do something about it. That’s what I learned.

	K:	Uh-huh. You enlisted in the Navy and when you got out after what, - two or three years?

	A:	Let me see, I was enlisted from March 3, 1978 through July 9, 1979. I got commissioned in 1979.

	K:	So you were just in for two years?



	A:	I was in twelve years, seven months; but that included commissioned time I was enlisted from 1978 to 1979 but I was commissioned from 1979 through when I got out.

	K:	Which was?



	A:	So I was commissioned, I think, July 1979 - then I got out of active duty October 1, 1990.

	K:	Okay so that was . . .



	A:	Twelve years, seven months, twenty-five days and four or five hours. [Laughter] K:	And your number was?





	A:	Still got my service number [inaudible 20:27]



	K:	Okay. All that was quite a while; I just didn’t realize that it was that long.



	A:	Yes, I was called a mustang; it is a formal list of who was a commissioned officer. K:	Then were you in the reserves or . . .?

	A:	No. This was all active.



	K:	Active. Okay, so then what – and this is probably a long story - but what were you trained in and what did you do, in effect?

	A:	Well, I [inaudible 21:06] cause the Navy is real good about something. Even when I was enlisted, no matter what your specialty is they make you a jack-of-all- trades. So that when I was enlisted I was training to be a fire control missiles technician – work on missiles, radars, surface to air missiles, and after [inaudible 21:21] stuff like that. Once I got commissioned I had several jobs; I was an educational services officer in the Navy a Junior Officer. Then from there I became a – I had three jobs; I went to the Seabees; I was a Headquarters Company Commander. I was also at the same time the Personnel Officer (a full time job) and I was also the Legal Officer. They sent me to eight weeks schooling in Newport, Rhode Island – boom, boom, boom bam, the next thing you know I’m full speed dealing with these lawyers who went to law school as a legal officer, because a legal in the Navy; I was in a forward deployed battalion. We go overseas if a war broke out, the Seabees a forward battalion would be the first ones to go hit the beach. So we had to be a self-contained unit; that’s why we had dentist, we had doctors, we had everything like a small city, so here I was





the legal officer cause when you are overseas in different countries you have civil matters cause sailors will go out and tear up the neighborhoods and you have to get them out of jail - coming back home, we had civil matters where our sailors did crimes sometimes – they owed alimony – I had to enforce all of that. Through the grace of God even though I only had eight weeks training I never lost a case in three years defending the command against the lawyers who went to law school and passed the bar. That was my job and I did it well.

	K:	Yes, it certainly sounds like it.



	A:	I was correct now don’t get me wrong, if it was wrong, I wouldn’t do it. So one time I got chastised by the commander officer said I was a “Trooper Officer” which meant that I wouldn’t do what the command said but I was leaning toward the troops. No ma’am, by no means - see me being former enlisted; it’s like being a school teacher and a kid. I asked my mama, “Why don’t you ever believe me; the teacher was lying sometimes.” So that being said, the enlisted guy’s not always wrong because he is enlisted. Sometimes it’s a leadership issue. So I looked at all sides.

	K:	And how long were you in that position? A:	Let’s see; a little over three years.

	K:	Oh, okay.



	A:	I had all three jobs for three jobs but I never lost a case. K:	Sounds like a fairly long time.





	A:	Yes, very hard work, but in the Seabees on deployment we had fifty hour weeks; got Sunday off. But that’s the way it is and it was unaccompanied; my wife and my son never went with me ‘cause we went to places where they didn’t let families go. Cause people didn’t like us.

	K:	Where did you go?



	A:	We went to Rhoda, Spain, my first deployment in [19]83 to [19]84; Okinawa, Japan [19]84 – [19]85 and then Porto Rico [19]85 – [19]86; and that’s when I left and went to other jobs. I say they didn’t like us because, we weren’t at war, but the military is not too well liked overseas, and they prey on you and they try and get you in trouble or they try to attack you and stuff like that.

	K:	I don’t know about that much. A:	It happens.

	K:	But that’s what I am learning.



	A:	It is partly our fault sometimes cause sometimes we are not too appreciative of the cultures that we can’t just go in there and expect somebody to be American with our values and our culture; don’t try to speak the language but treat them bad and I’ve seen a lot of that. You’ve got to respect people.

	K:	It sounds like the great adventure you wanted to travel and you did.



	A:	Well to be honest I didn’t really want to travel. It was all right until my son was born two months into Officer Candidate School, and then my wife brought him up when I graduated in [19]79 and he was two months old and I looked at him and I





told my wife, “I can’t do this” she I would never do a career I said “I can’t leave him”. Then they started shipping me out and it just started bothering me, you know. I still did my job but it always hurt me. That’s why a lot of people – why you did twelve years seven months – you should have stayed inside. I couldn’t stand leaving my family; it really hurt me. You know I love my country, but once I saw his little face I couldn’t . . .

	K:	And where were they living, in Florida?



	A:	She was living in Florida when I was in Officer Candidate School, in Newport, Rhode Island. Bless her heart, she took a bus and came up there to see me when I graduated. And I just decided - and every time I travelled from where they had to stay back at home; when I went overseas they stayed in Gulf Port Mississippi in base housing. I have no regrets; I served my country but every time I went overseas it would bother me. In order to do my job I had to put my son out of my mind and people [inaudible 26:25] ‘cause I would be heart sick the whole time on a ten month deployment. You can’t do your job and be combat ready if you have to – the problem is, I found out the more I did that, when I came home it was hard for me to get him back in my mind I didn’t feel right being home, I said “Nah, I’m not going to do this.” I am not just going to be around my son and don’t feel comfortable, so I decided right then I’m not going to stay in.

	K:	And you had more than one son?



	A:	Had one son and a daughter; my son is – he’ll be thirty-nine in four days and my daughter is twenty-nine.





	K:	Okay.



	A:	Why the gap? ‘Cause I told my wife I don’t want to have babies while I was deployed.

	K:	I think you have already anticipated another question, because I was going to ask you what was the hardest part of the military lifestyle to adapt to? And being away from your child I would say its right there.

	A:	It almost destroyed me cause, the way I looked at my son is the way I was hoping he would look at me like I used to look at my pops. My pops had an eighth grade education; drove trucks for Pan Am but the thing I remember about him most, no matter what he worked two jobs – my mom worked two jobs. We weren’t poor we were just poverty livable. No matter how tired he was working two jobs - as a kid you don’t think about how the dead woods and being in the swamps, earthworms. He’d get off that work bus, he’d see me standing with two fishing poles and a can of worms and he never denied me no matter how tired he was – he never denied me to go fishing with him.

	K:	That is remarkable.



	A:	So, I just wanted my son – I just didn’t want to leave him. Only time I left was when they made me leave him. Otherwise – oh, then when I got out in October they offered me some time in the reserves ‘cause back then even in October 1990 First Iraq War kicked off and they wanted me to come back in cause I had combat training and they offered me fifteen thousand dollars to sign back up and then they pay me my rank. I told them “No”. I said, what you all going to do is get





me back in on a reserve commission, activate me again and send me over there with my family. I’m not doing it. The reason; I was thirty-five, I think – I was too old to make me compulsory. So it’s not I shirked my duty. I gave them my life, I gave them my duties. I gave them more than I should have. I was at an age they couldn’t force me. I told my son – I promised I would never leave him again. So my word is more important to my son than to that.

	K:	Yep, so was there any particular part of your military duty that affected you – that had a direct effect on you when you got out?

	A:	I will say yes, about everything - for instance the lady over there across the street was shocked, she says you’re early, and I said no ma’am I’m here on time, I said Marie said to be here by three-thirty to sign some papers and she said you’re good, and I said no ma’am I’m military. I ain’t perfect, but the military, you had to be where you supposed to be, when you’re supposed to be, how you’re supposed to be. I wasn’t perfect all the time, especially after I got commissioned. If you showed up – you supposed to show up fifteen minutes early no matter where you supposed to be. If you showed up on time you was too late. So what I learned out of that is discipline and what I do, what I say, what I create, what I write, even if no one’s looking there is a spirit of excellence. You have to strive to be excellent. And I’ve been out since 1990, but I tell people when they say I appreciate your service, and I say, I still serve I still hold the doors open for people. I’m not perfect I still try to be nice, let people pass; the military teaches you discipline and respect for others. A team concept – people are too much of an individual nowadays and personally, I think everybody should do two years in





the military – compulsory. Teach them discipline and respect and stop complaining but do something. Sounds hard, but discipline – I just love discipline. I’m not a tyrant you know that, like right now I try to carry myself a certain way; be respectful.

	K:	Something to aim for and you seem to have reached it. Did you face any particular challenges when you were in the Navy?

	A:	I was kind of hoping to avoid it, but I guess since you ask that – well, it’s my opinion – I don’t know if it is true now, but in my opinion the Navy is probably one of the branches of the service that at that time has some growing up to do. I was in advanced electronics – back then, even in Alex Haley’s time, Blacks and other minorities were supposed to be ship service men – they do laundry, they’re cooks they do custodial things and it’s not bragging Marie, but out of a hundred, one hundred points, the most you can get on the ASMAT test, in 1978 I scored ninety-nine. Then they kind of wondered around did I cheat - you can’t cheat in those things – I never cheated. So I was in advanced electronics program and people wanted to know why I am I there. Well, I scored high enough; that is a very specialized program. I made eighty-four in school because of my grades. I graduated second in my class in the advanced electronics school; intensive two year training. Once I got commissioned it’s just like, wow, it got really bad. There were some good naval officers, but some of them were very bigoted – openly and overtly. What’s the word under the [inaudible 32:43] clause? When I was the legal officer, it really was profound when I was the legal officer. I wrote court martial papers for the commanding officer to sign to go against and to present





		officers and lawyers. Several times, those lawyers from the base JAG offices would come and see who wrote those charges; who authored the information. I said: “I did.” They said, “No, who really did?”	I said: “I did” Here I’m a full (wear two bars) O3, sitting in the office next to the executive officer. The command officer, the executive officer, then me; and they are going to ask me that. So I didn’t get ugly, I just let them know what time it is. I said: “I did” and then I beat them. [Laughter] There’s been times as a naval officer the word didn’t bother me I’m going to say that several time in the Seabees, I had a lot of guys that do drugs that do violence and I had to bust them because I had to do non-judicial punishment; take their rank, take their money, sometimes I had to kick them out of the Navy. Several times in my office there full uniform, the guy says, I don’t have to listen to no nigger officer. The word don’t bother me; I just looked at him and I said you’re dumb being a nigger	it means ignorant – you are very ignorant cause I am a Navy Lieutenant you are E 5, whatever, I have your life in my hands and going to be your advocate or your detriment. I never let the name calling be a detriment; what the charges are is what they deserved. So I was fair and equitable and never took their pride, but if they did two times in a row I had to put them out. It’s stuff like that; the other officers would come and take my stuff, one thing that really hurt me when we were on deployment all the E 7s, that is enlisted guys and above, including the commanding officers all of them had access to military vehicles after hours to be able to go into town and go places.

Out of that battalion I was the only one E 7 or above, which was probably about fifty of them, that didn’t have access to a vehicle – my own vehicle; I had to go





borrow some. So what happened, thank God, the enlisted troops saw that – White and Black – they saw it. “Mr. Adams, do you need a ride?” They’d come by and get me. So good things and bad things and then the officers got mad because – you’re hanging out with the troops, well I said, “Who else going to help me?” So to make a long story short what was probably designed to discourage me only encouraged me. No matter how much they push I was going to be better than what they thought I was; and it made me a better person. I just learned that in a deficit if all you are going to do is feed me rocks, I’m going to be the only one getting fat. [Laughter]
