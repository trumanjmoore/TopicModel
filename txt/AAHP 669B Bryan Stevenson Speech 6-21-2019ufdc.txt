	A:	It is a great honor to introduce Bryan Stevenson! [Applause]

	A:	Mr. Stevenson is the founder and Executive Director of the Equal Justice Initiative in Montgomery, Alabama. He is a public interest lawyer who has made significant strides throughout his thirty plus year career. Challenging the death penalty, inhumane prison conditions, and end of life sentencing applied to adults and children. Under his guidance the EJI team has achieved reversals, relief, and release of over a hundred twenty-five prisoners on Alabama’s death row.

[Applause]



	A:	Many of you in this room probably know Mr. Stevenson through the institutions he opened in April of 2018, The National Memorial for Peace and Justice, and the Legacy Museum. These faces clarify the connections between the horrors of enslavement, racial lynchings, and Jim Crow segregation. Pushing all of us to reckon the current narratives of racial difference in the United States. In the mass incarceration of people of color. Over 500,000 people have visited these sights since they opened last April, with many more planning to visit this summer. In recognition of his work, Mr. Stevenson has received numerous honors and awards, including the National Legal Aid and Defender of Life Achievement Award, The Forward Foundation Visionary’s Award, and the McArthur Foundation Genius Prize. As a part of his advocacy of work, he is also an author of the critically acclaimed New York Times bestselling book, Just Mercy.

I came to Orlando when I was about ten years, ten or eleven years old, after they had just opened up the new Disney World. And I grew up in a poor community and my father was a domestic, he cleaned other people’s homes, and I’ll be honest, growing up in the segregated community it was at times frustrating and angering, the things we couldn’t do. Then when integration came, they closed the things that I’d been looking forward to. So my sister and I, that just to be honest had been preoccupied with getting in a swimming pool, we had never had a chance to get into a swimming pool and when integration came, instead of letting Black kids in the pool, they closed the public swimming pool in our community. As we got older I would go with my dad down to the beach where he would be cleaning these houses and I’d see these White kids enjoying the pool and everybody seemed to be having so much fun, and by the time I was ten, I’m not gonna lie, I had a thing about getting in a swimming pool.

[Laughter]

[Laughter]



	S:	So we would be ready for the swimming pool and we got on the bus and we started to drive and we came all the way down. My sister started screaming that she saw the pool and she ran over to me and said, “There’s the pool!” And as soon as the bus stopped we jumped off of that bus and we ran over to this swim pool and it was as big and as glorious as we had dreamed. It was like one of those pools in the movies, we were so excited. There were seven or eight White kids in the pool and their parents were sitting around the pool. We didn’t pay them any mind we were just thankful, and we were ready. We ripped of our clothes.

[Laughter]



S:   I looked at my sister I said, “I’m gonna count to three and we’re gonna jump high in the air into this pool” And we got on the edge of the pool and I held her by the hand and I counted to three and we jumped high in the air and we landed in that

And then I realized that chaos had broken out around us. The White parents of the kids were screaming at their children, “Get out of the pool.” And I watched these White mothers running to the pool and violently snatching their children out of the water. I couldn’t figure out what was going on ‘cause they were just screaming, “Get out of the water, get out of the water!” And I couldn’t figure out what was happening and I grabbed my sister by the hand because I was getting scared and I moved to the side of the pool. I watched this big man, this White man start walking towards the pool and he walked into the water and he walked over to the swimming pool and he snatched him by the arm and pulled him out of the pool.

When the little boy got pulled out the pool he started crying and I was so stunned by what was happening I did something I knew I wasn’t supposed to do, but as that man was leaving I couldn’t help but turn to him and I said, “What’s wrong?” And I never will forget what happened when I came to Orlando. That man looked at me and he said, “You’re wrong nigger.” It wasn’t the first time somebody had used that word on me but something about that setting that just shook me. I believe in the power of proximity. Many of us were taught that if there’s a bad part of town, you should stay as far away from the bad part of town as possible. Today I’m gonna argue the opposite, I believe we gotta get closer to the people living in our communities that are struggling, that are suffering, that are dealing with inequality, that are dealing with injustice. When you get proximate to people who are struggling, who are suffering, you hear things you can’t hear from a distance. You see things you can’t see from away. Our politicians sometimes fail us because they come up with policies that are created at a distance. You’ve gotta get proximate. And the problem we sometimes make is that we think we have to have it all together before we get proximate. No, you get it together when you are in proximity with the people you are trying to serve.
