AAHP 553 Dr. John Warford Interviewed by Daniela Lopez on March 22, 2018

1 hour 4 minutes | 32 pages



Abstract: In this interview Dr. Warford talks about his experiences as a doctoral student at the University of Florida. He was a member of the Loyal Order of the 99, and organizations that were meant to protect Black students against acts of racisms and to solidify their identity on campus. While being involved with the Loyal Order, Dr. Warford founded the Black Male Development Circle. This organization was meant to encourage and engage Black males from on and off campus. Dr. Waford has also has since moved to Tallahassee and is a part of the faculty of Florida A and M University.







Keywords: [African American History, Black Male Development Circle, University of Florida, Loyal Order of the 99, FAMU]

	L:	So this is Daniela Lopez with the Samuel Proctor Oral History Program. Today is March 22, 2018 and I’m here with Dr. John Warford. Hi, Dr. Warford.

	W:	How you doing?



	L:	So you know I’m researching Black student movements and organizations at UF around the time that you were there. But first I kind of want to get a bit of background about yourself. So, what year were you born and what led you to go to UF?

	W:	I was born in 1956. I’m from St. Louis, Missouri. I came to University of Florida because I wanted to work on a doctorate degree. I was living in Miami at the time—I lived there for about five years. And my first attempt was to maybe go to graduate school after FIU. I did master’s degree coursework at FIU to see if I could stay in Miami and do it there. But the degree of my professional and personal connections—I needed to be separated from them to the degree I could focus on the doctorate program. So that led me to the University of Florida and of course UF has got tremendous resources. So I came up to Gainesville from Miami having lived in Miami for about six years. That’s how I got there.

	L:	Okay, what were you studying?



	W:	Geography. I started in political science. I wanted to do international studies, international relations but it wasn’t enough of it there in the department so I knew within the first two months that I needed to transfer or maybe go back to Miami. I didn’t want to transfer back and go back home so I ended up going over to the





geography department—colloquiums, on Friday they would have talks. And I fell in love with it. I’m an interdisciplinary creature by nature in terms of academics and studying. So, it was a perfect fit. It was a small department. My colleagues and classmates were great. So, that’s how I stayed in Turlington and stayed at the University of Florida.

	L:	Cool and what years did you attend, if you could give generally?



	W:	Oh, okay. That I’m going to have to get back with you. I know I came here in 1999 to be a professor at the business school. So I probably have to go back to almost 1990 when I first came because I lived in Gainesville all of eight years. And then in 19]98-[19]99 I came to Tallahassee. So I’m kind of ball-parking that right now. As we kind of discussed earlier, I’m not real good with years sometimes I have to look stuff up myself. So it was clearly in the [19]90s, the early [19]90s.

	L:	So how would you describe the environment in the early [19]90s at UF? In terms of—

	W:	My view of course was as a graduate student but if you are in Turlington you’re right next to the set. So it’s one of the main hubs of campus in campus life. I met wonderful people from everywhere. Brilliant people and I had a great experience. There was a time where I think some vestiges of some old mentalities was still there but the students were full of promise, the students were full of support for each other. Now this was the period before cell phones and other things that kind of allow people to filter their experiences or even how we communicate with each other. So, we were very engaged, all of the students. And not just around sports,





around all types of issues. I’m sure it was not the highly politicized environment where Black men in prior years or across the United States you know, students and students organizations, activism, issues and confrontations with administration. It wasn’t inherently that. I think we did find ourselves with sometimes, as Black students, was dealing with definitely unfair treatment or some of the narrow attitudes and the callousness how sometimes how some teachers and professors dealt with students in general, maybe women in general, or specifically, or minority students, Black students. It was not across the board, right, but those things did exist. I came as a graduate student and I had a wonderful experience. I never had any overt racial issues in the classroom or outside of it. You know, personalities being what they are, people are what they are but fortunately I came at a very mature time and I could stay focused. But I knew that was a source of contention with some students in some departments, some areas that were traditionally White male driven with students and so forth. So, issues of favoritism like you find every place was still in play. So women and maybe minority students they had to push, many of them not in every case, they had to push a little harder or they had to make decisions about whether it was a good fit for them and if they could finds some people in their area at the University to kind of support them. But one good thing about—many good things at UF—that we had some supportive people in student affairs and in student government to a degree. I would say student government, they had a broad range of interests that they were trying to address but we had representation in places like student government and in La Casita and also with the IBC, the





Institute of Black Culture. So we had supportive faculty and administrators and staff. So, we relied on them and other folks who may not have been of our ethnicity or our race to encourage us and guide us. So, we relied on those things. With me coming, I came at a time when certain people were already in place. So dynamics were already what they were and this is how I came to notice the work of Nikitah Imani and David Padgett and Stanley Harrison. My first semester there, my first year there, I was observing. I just watched. I talked to people but my main goal was to make sure I could prove I could stay there. So I stayed focused on school and so forth but just being the person that I am I’m naturally drawn to help students because I have a student affairs background. So that’s just me. I use that to this day right here at FAMU working with students and I think about the whole person and not just how they may be performing in class and what it looks like with their grades. So, the Royal Order of the Ninety-nine were already established when I got there and I learned about these brothers and the students who were positively impacted by them. I want to say this about them, to a man. I think that—I had a young lady, she might have been dating Nikitah Imani at the time, so it was not all guys. It was a little small cadre of folks. They wore berets and leather jackets, very similar of the Panthers but these were brilliant people. Every one of them and they loved the opportunities and what University of Florida offered them. So they were not anti-UF, they were just pro- students. People could come to them to tell them what was going on and what was happening. So these were not part-time students and full-time activists who just kind of hung around the fringes and just see what kind of trouble they came





in. We’re talking about PhD students. We’re talking about people majoring in business and finance. That along with their passion to want to see students have the best experiences possible there at the school. And they were very service oriented. That drove me to them. Nikitah Imani and Dave Padgett and Stanley Harrison in particularly, just the brilliance of these guys, I was drawn to that. So, we had many good conversations and many good things came from that. In fact, the founding of the Black Male Development Circle came from a summer—it might have been in the spring, workshop of student empowerment that Keyman, of the Royal Ninety-nine, put on for the students. From that, a series of conversations with David Padgett and Nikitah Imani—and I was kind of stuck in my school work and I needed to do something that I had full control over, that empowered me. And that summer I put together a program that we ran for three years and we got to a point very soon after we initiated it, we met every day—not every day but every week, called the Black Male Development Circle. So the people that I just mentioned and others like Phillip Tavernier, who was an undergraduate and he became one of our—or I think I want to say he was our orientation instructor, in fact he might have been the second director, I want to say Phillip Tavernier who lives in Miami of the Black Male Development Circle.

So we got to do a lot of positive programs in the city and on campus for the students. So, that came out of some of the work that David Padgett and Nikitah Imani were doing because they were holding these forums about “What are the things we can do to learn from each other and support each other?” So that now is part of the history of University of Florida. I think they call it something different





there at the University of Florida but it’s something that I think they’ve made it a part of what they do at the Institute of Black Culture for Black Students. About ten years ago I got to go back, maybe a little less, and meet the people who are still kind of keeping something going. I incorporated it and all that and found out later that it was something—young ladies, because they started a Black Female Development Circle a year after we started the male one and somebody is doing that down in Orlando. So, so many things came from that because so many leaders during that time, natural leaders were there at the University of Florida.

I’m sure that Dr. Imani told you about some of the things they used to do to get attention. There’s something about the role of drama in activism. You see it— people do these things with props, you people do this in congress and state legislatures and in our federal system. They will do demonstrations of some type to get people’s attention because there’s something about the role of drama that allows people to remember what is being done. Whether it’s done well or it’s corny. Whether it’s stacking up reams of paper to show the amount of legislation that people have to work through to try to understand an issue. Or their props.

I’m pretty sure he told you—and I never saw, I’m trying to think did I ever see this in a class of mine, no. But I heard about this. These guys would come in with caskets in the classroom.

	L:	Really?



	W:	Yes. They would talk about the death of respect or the death of common sense or the death of knowledge. They would come into a classroom with a casket and I think mainly unannounced. So the teacher would get caught off-guard. They of





course would get shooed out of there, “Look you got to get out of here.” But they would make their statement and there are a number of any other things that they did, right, that had people buzzing, “Wait a minute.” And these were not just slight, small, things that happened once and all of a sudden you got to deal with the Ninety-nine, you know, showing up at your door. These were ongoing things because just like every place during that time and earlier, you had people who were set in their ways and just dealt with people any kind of way they felt. Their own biases and bigotry would come out. You know a professor has a lot of influence if not power in that classroom and I know that from being in this seat.

So we have to be very sensitive of what are the needs the orientation of our students and we just can’t be callous. Sometimes some of the older professors were that. They weren’t inherently bad people. Some of them were just straight curmudgeons. They were just bitter old folks but we have had a history of that in many places right here at FAMU. We got people who have had these kind of attitudes and these approaches. And they are passed their time where they should have made adjustment if they are going relevant in the university. So we had that at UF. Like I said I never got sense it was rampant, but at that time, you know, women in certain fields, engineers, they were new. So they caught even more difficult times. And some departments rarely—it was never was a whole college but it was just big climb just to get through there or to stay. Because professors sometimes, we feel like we can say whatever comes to our mind and there’s no repercussion for that. So those are some of the instances that they would address but they would also do very proactive things, and these were





scholarly folks, who kept up. Also there was a person there in the sociology department. Dave Padgett was in geography. He was in geography before I got to geography department and we were the only two Black students in the geography department at that time. And I don’t think we had any in the undergrad department during that time. But Nikitah Imani was in sociology which was around the corner on third floor in Turlington. So I met Dr. Joe Fagan, highly celebrated sociologist and also a Dr. Hernan Vera. Dr. Fagan and University of Florida has the ability to attract some of the best scholars in the world. He had already made a career in sociology dealing with issues of racism, in particularly White racism. He’s a global expert on it, in particularly United States was his focus. And he, like a lot of people, came to the University of Florida, they were massive grant drawers. They could draw money and he had done that to University of Texas. I think when he left UF… he was still there when I left and moved to Tallahassee. I think he was going back to University of Texas. He no doubt is retired now. But these are people along with Hernan Vera. I think Hernan is Puerto Rican. They wrote books. Nikitah Imani wrote a book with them called “White Racism.” So that program and in particular Dr. Fagan. He would address issues of racial sensitivity most folks really didn’t want to touch. And he would seek out students to talk to us about these various things and our understanding of things. So we had professors at the University of Florida who wanted to know what we thought about certain things. Not just what they could share with us, right, and he was one of them. And I had people in my department who were also that way. And it wasn’t always inherently radical or revolutionary





or things like that, but they wanted to know what we thought, my major professors. They wanted to know what I thought and because of that, I was even more open to what they were sharing. So I really had some incredible professors there. What generated out of that had a lot to do with what fed these students.

I’m glad that you mentioned because he’s a White professor. So a lot of folk didn’t know what to do with him, but you know, he and other people are there in anthropology and other areas. They were just great people to know. Do you have any specific questions about any things I just mentioned or talking about?

But outside of the founder of the Black Male Development Circle, the Black Graduate Student Organization. I was president of the Black Graduate Student Organization for about two years. So I was a member of that. It was called BGSO. And Nikitah Imani was a member of that as were most Black graduate students. And were actually able host the Nationally Black Graduate Student Conference…. Was that [19]94? I think it was [19]94, in Gainesville at the University of Florida. So we were able to bring Black graduate students from around the country that came and the university did a beautiful job helping us facilitate that by hosting that it on campus. So those were the two main organizations because I really didn’t have time for other things. I was a member of a Greek letter organization but I could not be active, I kinda just supported them in what they did. I just supported the students in general. Nikitah Imani and other members, some named and not named, Shawn Jones and other people in engineering, all kinds of fields, archeology and Black Male Development Circle was a group and I probably got some literature I can share with you or you can take pictures of. It was designed to be a program from high school up to forty-five years old to address some of the broader issues in terms of holistic development. Whether just simple things of male-female relationships. We covered the gamut professional skills, personal hygiene, time management, things people already





knew. The whole idea was every member was not only a student but a teacher. So we found what people strengths were and we encouraged people to do presentations. So me, Nikitah Imani, or other people, we didn’t do all of them, right, Terry Wike. We encouraged people to come up with ideas based on the format that we found kinda like an area or module we could put it in and we got to a point where we met every week so the Million Man March, we were up and running. We took people to the Million Man March. In fact we coordinated a busload of people from the community. Black man, from the community and from the university to go to the Million Man March.

	L:	What was the Million Man March?



	W:	The Million Man March was the first of its kind in modern times where different constituent groups would march on Washington. We were far from the first ones you know back in Civil Rights days you had to the poor people’s march and you had other throngs of people to go to the march. Well, the Million Man March, from a group of concerned community leaders on a national level proposed tje idea that we go to Washington DC and you can find a lot out about the Million Man March. And this was to get a million Black men to come to DC. Not only to hear speeches but to talk about our issues and meet each other. And I’m not being boastful, but to be a part of that the world stopped. Because more than a million

But that was a time when student government looked like every aspect of student life knew about it or was some degree affected. And the Black students were kinda at the center of this while they just doing they school work and keep going. But sometimes when you tell young people “You shouldn’t do a thing.” Oh they can’t wait to do it. So that’s part of the reason out of curiosity were just there. L:  To what degree do you think more national politics or issues sort of arose within the school, you know I mean? In terms if there was protest or something happening nationally that they can use. Would it affect the students?

W:  During those times, oh yeah, yeah. That whole thing about Dr. Khalid. Because that went national his speech, and various different people from different from different sides. You know they figure out “How this serve our agenda?” That’s what people do in politics. So that elevates that thing and the more attention it gets. This is before the days of cellphones and Twitter. Cellphones were just starting to come and everybody didn’t have one. But there was definitely no Tweeting, no Facebook, no Myspace. These things didn’t exist. So you found out things through national news conversation. No blogs. Those things didn’t exist then. So it was word of mouth and you talk to people. In Gainesville, the university is in a place where there is a relatively strong degree of conservatism. And some people say Right Wing and I guess that’s a fair statement. But you have moderates that live there. But this is a college town. It’s a college environment in a place that has been very strongly pro-republican, pro-guns, pro-life. And this is just happened where the University of Florida sits and we get students from around Georgia and Florida to come to UF because it is a flagship institution. It’s got tremendous resources. So we get students across the political spectrum definitely students who may be conservative because of their upbringing. And because of their only exposures. I think every place is politicized. I guess it’s to a degree in which there is a certain





degree of clashing because there is not more of a uniformity. The more diversity you may have to deal with that’s open, the more likely there can be some open tension. But people kinda moving in the same direction having kinda the same view. You have to dig a little deeper to find things that stand out. And then, you know it was a big campus, a big school. We were all right in pretty much the same place. So it wasn’t a thing to my knowledge that I remember. There was this constant open battles, sitting in here and sitting in. Like I say, just from starting with Dr. Lombardi we had a president that we knew cared about us and we could go to him with our problems. That meant everything. Whether people disagreed or not that we knew about John Lombardi and ultimately something he said end up becoming a big issue, a big political football and he end up leaving. I think he became the president of the University of Massachusetts. And then we also had Dr. Sorensen, he was the Provost at that time. Another person, very reasonable, very polish ordained man and he’s very open minded. Dr. Andrew Sorensen was the Provost’s name mainly during that time. And this is somebody else we could talk to and he care about the students.

That’s mainly because of that and the pressure was on the president. It was a young lady. So the guys, we said, “Look we are just going to figure out how she going to get to A and B. Because some people were calling her on her phone.” Now there may have been… there probably were cellphones then but it wasn’t like it was now. So she was getting threats. Excuse me. The overt thing that I remember. I am hard pressed right now to think of other things. Now some of that may have been settled down. If that was taken place before I got there and that may have been a reason for the Royal Order of the 99 in the beginning. It was a response to something that was going on that was even stronger before my time. That’s something you might want to consider.

L:  I see. A lot of the issues you brought up you have brought up are stuff that have come back. Like whether or not to have speakers on campus and possible resurgence of almost a White nationalism. Do you feel like students nowadays have the same sort of ability to collectivize as they did before?

W:  It’s more complicated now. L:  It is?

W:  Yeah, I would say so because I’m not sure of the range of students who come to school now, how aware socially and politically and where they are. There may be some but they have such demands on them to stay focused on what they are doing. And we still have many first generation Black students and minority students come to the university. And some are just, their interests are elsewhere. And they may respond collectively to some things but all of these things are possible because there’s a space where you believe you’re safe and you can be supported in being different. I think many people will turn stuff over to Black Lives Matter which I don’t know how much people in Black Lives Matter, not all them, ‘cause we can’t speak of them as a group, how much they understand what has proceeded them. In terms of their approach towards things and you always have people who their primary focus is to agitate and not to solve problems. So if you got people parachuting in, that’s usually not good because they may start something that the students who are there have to contend with. So I’m not on the ground in places like this but I think it will be different because a lot of our students across the board





now, they just trying to deal with what’s right in front of them. And they don’t want any issues. Now some of them, they come from an expectation they have speak up and speak out, but it depends on what they seen in their lives. Individually and if they come from a legacy where people have been empowered whether it’s for Gay Rights, whatever. If they come from that, then they gonna know “Okay, I need to say something or do something.” But usually it has to be a ground swell of a few people. It gets so big that other people can’t ignore it and young people are “What’s happening?” people. Okay that’s what’s happening? I need to be over there supporting that or at least looking at that. So nowadays because of the demands on students, many of them probably work more than ever before. Along with job, along with school, they don’t see themselves as having the time if they have the inclination to be more supportive of these things. I’m not dismissing that but I think it’s a lot more complicated now for everybody. I do think there are people who want to make sure that our state institutions reflect what they look like and if they feel like they have the stronger hand or they are the majority view of the thing or the more popular view they want to press what they see as an advantage. I can understand that but it may not be best for the overall health of the institution if it means you have to negate or deny other people a safe place to be who they are.

L:   I see.



W:  And I think that’s an ongoing thing in a lot of places. And because the University of Florida is so special, it’s always going to attract brilliant people but we are affected at UF because of the general climate on a national level in part of what the environment holds. It’s not so different from the liberal environment of Berkley,





California, and I’ve been to Oakland, Berkley. I’ve visited Oakland. I’ve got some mentors that live Northern California. And it’s just a way people do thing there that’s in the air. So you can have a different view but you are always aware that this is a place, you know, liberals may do certain things just ‘cause ‘”hell we can do it.” And you’re like, “Well does that make any sense?’ “Yeah, this what we do, this is Berkley, don’t you know? This is Berkley.” So, you know, you can reverse that and you can see this in other places where people say, “Well this is what this place is about.” But of course being a state institution, it’s not like any other place and it’s got an obligation, not to put itself in a bad position. So even if it just has to rely on legal issues to keep a more open society in the campus, then a university would do that.

L:  Do you have any suggestions for students who want to make a real change? Not just to shout because they can but really do something to help and affect other students?

W:  I would say this and I learned this from— I studied motivational tapes and I studied this one particular one’s Bryan Tracey and I learned this from these old cassette tapes I had. And Mahatma Gandhi said the same, “Be the change that you want to see in the world. Be that yourself.” So I would say to students, “What kind of campus would my campus be if everybody in it was just like me?” What kind of dorm would my dorm be if everybody in it was just like me? What kind of university would my university be if everybody in it was just like me?” So all of us have to do a degree of self-reflection if not interrogation as to why we show up in the world the way we do and what we really hold onto in our values systems. Is that a double





standard compared to what we ask in other people to be about? So if I can’t do something to unify my own body, mind, and spirit to show up as a more genuine, compassionate, understanding, intelligent person on a day-to-day basis, how can I demand that from someone else when I can’t reflect it? So I would say that with any student, for whatever cause they think because sometimes complaining and unfortunate… A lot of people nowadays could be social media. They could be Facebook activists, things like that. We can shout how terrible it is and just go right on back to not changing or doing anything about it. So it’s the danger of wanting to make an impact without doing the work. You climb the mountain. You see the flags that says “Impact” at the top of the mountain and everybody wants to make an impact but they don’t see the mountain. They just see the summit. Simon Sinek this fellow who did this YouTube video and it has actually been animated. It’s only about thirteen minutes long. I’d encourage you, Ms. Lopez, to look at that. It’s called “Millennials in the Workplace” by Simon Sinek, S-I-N-E-K. It’s brilliant and he talks about at this stage the millennials and through no fault of their own, the things that have proven to be obstacles once they leave the university because of the conditioning. And one of them is in patience based on the fact that you can have a filtered view of how somebody has gotten to where they are. You don’t see the long work that goes into building these trust relationships that actually makes change. And that‘s part of what we are talking about with the 99 and all of these folks. We built up trust relationships with them just as people, regardless of their background, where they came from, their race. My major professor Eric Moleky other people, I mean, I respect them for who they are and who they show up every





day. That has taught me how to be a better person, a better student, better professor. So if I see the summit, I see the impact I want to make. Can I go through the process that gets me the outcome I want? See that is the biggest challenge right now. Am I prepared to go through the process? So that is what I would encourage the students to do. Most of life is about learning how to get out our own way and if you got an idea of something that needs to be better, that means you have tremendous potential to do something toward it. But I just can’t pay lip service to it. And I got to have a realistic understanding about how change comes about. It doesn’t come about quick, it doesn’t come about instantly and it’s very arduous, but it’s all tied to relationships and how I show up. So I would just wish them the best. Be willing to take advice before you give it and know that many things you think haven’t been figured out or never been done, they’ve been done already.
