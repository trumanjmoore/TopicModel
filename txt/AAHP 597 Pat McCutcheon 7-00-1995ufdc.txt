AAHP 597 Pat McCutcheon 7-00-1995 Interviewed by Jannet Snyder Mathews in July 1995

26 minutes | 15 pages



Abstract: In this interview, Janet Snyder Mathews sits down with Black Lee County elder Pat McCutcheon to discuss his life in Fort Meyers, Florida. McCutcheon attended the Williams Academy in the mid-1920s, one of the most acclaimed schools for Black students in the state. He then attended Dunbar Academy, run by his father’s relative Professor Dixon. He recalls the layout of the school for Mathews, remembering the location of various features like doorways and staircases.





Keywords: [Lee County, Florida, Black community, community history, Dunbar School, Williams Academy, segregation, education]

	MA:	And when were you born Mr. McCutcheon? MC:	October 25, 1921.

	MA:	M’kay and when did you attend the Williams Academy? What year or how old were you?

	MC:	Let’s see, I started to attend it in 1925 I think it was. MA:	And how long were you there?

	MC:	Hmm?



	MA:	From what grade to what grade?



	MC:	First grade, second grade, and then I went over to Dunbar. MA:	You were born in 1921?

	MC:	Mm-hmm.



	MA:	You were only four years old?



	MC:	Yeah four or five years old, somewhere along about 1925 or [19]26; somewhere along there.

	MA:	It’s probably [19]26. MC:	Yeah.

	MA:	And you went there how many grades?



	MC:	That’s my wife there if you see somebody walking. MA:	I guess. I talked to her many a time.





	MC:	Oh, okay.



	MA:	You went there from what? First grade to what grade? MC:	First grade, second grade—

	MA:	First, second.



	MC:	I went to third grade at Dunbar.



	MA:	All right, so it was probably 1926 to 1927. MC:	Yeah, because Dunbar didn’t open ‘til 1927.

	MA:	Okay. These are the drawings I’ve made so far if you want to look at them and tell me if any of those seem familiar to you, or if you have totally different—

	MC:	Can’t tell you about a floor plan, nothin’ about a building now, a building— [Laughter]—more than sixty-years old. It’s been gone over, renovated, and redone.

	MA:	‘Cause these are about sixteen different drawings and everyone has a different opinion. Really a beautiful house.

	MC:	Thank you. Now this school was a one-floor building before it became a two-floor building. The second floor was added to it. It was a single for—

	MA:	When was the second floor added?



	MC:	It might have been—let’s see Horace around 1924, so that’d mean me and him go to school about 1930. In 1930, I was in Dunbar.

	MA:	It was first a one-story?



	MC:	Yeah. No, that second floor was there when I went to school there, too.



	MA:	Yeah, that’s what I thought. Could you just go through all of them and maybe one is very similar because there’s so many different? I have them in alphabetical





order so… Not all of them are that accurate, or what I think is accurate, because they’re so different. And some of them are very similar and some of them are dissimilar.

	MC:	There was not a hallway. I don’t remember a hallway. MA:	On the first floor?

	MC:	Yeah all this was open and had a porch here in the front. And the porch—



	MA:	Okay so if I’m on Cranford—I’m gonna do the first floor first—okay there’s a porch.

	MC:	Yeah.



	MA:	And was there a railing around the porch? MC:	No.

MA:  How did you get up the steps? I mean where would the steps be? Were they on the right, the left, or the center?

MC:  The steps was on here near the center, because this is where we came in, but the whole building was an open building. That first floor in there, it was four full rooms divided up.

	MA:	So you go in, this is the porch. Was there a roof overhang on the porch? MC:	Yeah. When you came into the building, yes.

	MA:	Right smack-dab in the center? MC:	Yeah.

	MA:	And was there a single door or double door?



MC:  Well it was wider than an ordinary single door, so then it coulda been a… It was a double door.





	MA:	It was a double door, okay.



	MC:	This porch came round here, too. MA:	This porch came around here?

	MC:	Mmhm.



	MA:	And what was over here? Was there a sign? MC:	Nothin’ but an entrance in here.

	MA:	There was an entrance here? MC:	Yeah.

	MA:	Right here, too?



	MC:	Mm-hmm. And when the second floor was there, there was steps here at the back going up to the second floor.

	MA:	And did the steps go around the building or did they go out the building? MC:	They’re going out.

	MA:	They went out like this? MC:	Mm-hmm.

	MA:	So then there’s a door right here on the second floor. [Sounds of sketching and moving papers] So…okay. Second, first. And was there a side building right here? I heard there’s a side building, quite a few people remember that. I’ll show you what—

	MC:	If it was I don’t know anything about it, and I remember as good as anybody else does. There was a porch there. There was not another building, there was not another building.





	MA:	So there’s a side porch, so you could walk all the way around the side of the building?

	MC:	Yeah. That’s what they must be confused with. It was not another building. There was only one building.

MA: This is what I have [Presents McCutcheon with a Sanborn Fire Insurance Map]. This is from a fire insurance drawing in 1926. It had this and then they… This is Lemon and Cranford. It looks like it has back steps here.

	MC:	Yeah.



	MA:	This looks like a porch, and this looks like a side— MC:	That was part of that porch. That was not a building.

	MA:	That was not a building? So it was never a principal’s office? MC:	No, everything was right in the main building.

	MA:	Okay, and this was two stories, and there was no hallway on the first floor?



	MC:	I can’t remember a hallway. I can remember all of us entering, but we would divide up, because there were no partition walls.

	MA:	There were no partition walls?



	MC:	Nope, they divided the classes. There were bearing walls to get a second floor. MA:	There’s what walls?

	MC:	Bearing walls. MA:	Okay.

MC: This is what’s called a bearing wall here that goes to roof on the house along the superstructure.





	MA:	Right, so just the side walls. And what was in the first floor? Were there any — like the teachers’ desks and the windows? Do you remember where that was?

	MC:	No. Janie got this…



	MA:	She also had an interior staircase. MC:	Right, yeah.

	MA:	Do you remember an interior staircase?



	MC:	I’m trying to figure out now where that interior staircase was that leads from the first floor to the second floor. And this could be—

	MA:	I’ll show you some other ones. Some people had here [pulls out more sketches].



And then some in the back.



	MC:	That’s the back, it was a back stair, but it… It came up to the building like this.



	MA:	But see, this person also has this little room here getting in here and then also a back staircase that went up the gap. So there was an interior and an exterior staircase.

	MC:	There’s no such thing as an auditorium or chapel. It was one big room.



Everything was in.



	MA:	Well he said the auditorium chapel, he said that’s—



	MC:	No, he’s confused with Dunbar. There was an auditorium and a chapel, it was Dunbar, one big room.

	MA:	Just one big room upstairs? Chapel? And do you remember if there’s a stage up there?

	MC:	No.



	MA:	Was there anything up there?







MC:

No. None of that was in this school.

No stage.

MC:

Yeah.

MA:

MC:

This is the back where they sat. The teacher’s backs were to the back.

MA:

Okay, so they were over here. And then this desk right here, how many people



were up—were you allowed a desk here?





	MC:	It was just enough for the students. MA:	How many students?

	MC:	Well not for each student, because some students sat two to a seat. It was a desk with a folding seat in it.

	MA:	And there’s this desk and the teacher’s desk, there’s nothing else up there.



	MC:	Yeah, it was space between the classes, each side of the room or however the teachers arranged ‘em you see? At that time the desks were not fastened to the floor like we did have in later years over at Dunbar where they fastened the desks; screwed in. They could be moved about; they would move the desks.

	MA:	Do you remember what the exterior of the building was painted? The outside?



	MC:	A battleship grey. Now there wasn’t many windows in that building if there were any windows at all.

	MA:	Do you remember where the windows are? MC:	There was a window in the back.

	MA:	Right here? MC:	I remember.

	MA:	This is the first floor. This is the second.



	MC:	Yeah. I don’t remember any windows being on the sides of the building, nor in the front. Just that opening in that front where we went in after we came up out of the yard, onto the porch, into the school.

	MA:	So there’s a window here on the front? MC:	No, just the door.

	MA:	Oh, just a door had a window on it.







MC:



MA:

No, it was a door opening.



Okay.

No windows, but there were windows later on cut into it.

MA:

Where were those windows?

MC:

They splattered around with a tool.

MA:

MC:

Yeah.

MA:

So was there one over? Right there?

MC:

Yeah, but there was no window from the porch to the building from here. Unh-uh



no window.

MC:

It went to the side as far as they built it and that’s where it stopped. But it was all

MC:

MC:

MC:

I can’t tell you, but I remember the exterior was painted a battleship grey.

MC:

In front of each class and all classes faced east from west.

MA:

MC:

The classes faced like this. The backs was to the west, faces to the east.

MA:

teachers is in front, of course the teacher’s gonna walk around anyway.

MA:

MC:

First, second, primer.

MA:

MC:

Primer, first, and second and the teachers would divide it up like they wanted. No



partitions in there.





MA: And everybody was just in one big room? MC:  Yeah.

MA:  And you were able to hear above all that?



MC: Yeah. Like a general assembly room, they were far enough apart and for the amount of kids, they had good hearing. The classes were usually far enough apart.

MA: And you don’t remember a side addition, you don’t remember a side here? MC:  No, I don’t remember.

MA:  All right.



	MC:	Now upstairs there were partitions between—it was just a straight hall upstairs.



The building was divided.



	MA:	Where was the hallway upstairs?



	MC:	Running from east to west. That was the structure of the building. MA:	So, like down here. So there’s a wall right here?

	MC:	Mm-hmm. MA:	Like that?

	MC:	Yeah, this divided the building.



	MA:	And there were like two classrooms? MC:	Yeah.

	MA:	Okay and did they have two teachers’ desks here again or just one?



	MC:	They had as many teachers’ desks as there was classes that they needed up there. If they needed two classes up there that’s how many teachers’ desks. If they needed three, you had that many.





	MA:	Okay because they have a wall dividing then that makes two. So we’ll have two teachers’ desks—that’s what I’m trying to clarify—right down the middle of it.

	MC:	Some of these things I’m not going to recall after sixty years.



	MA:	I understand. I want you to just do the best you can. If you don’t remember just say you don’t remember, and I’ll respect that. It’s fine with me.

	MC:	But I never know anything about this principal’s office being on the side, another building. I know there was a porch that went around it.

	MA:	Do you remember if the windows on the first floor matched the second floor? MC:	Can’t tell whether it was directly inline or not.

MA: But there were windows on the second floor? MC: Yeah. One or two, three, something like that. [Moves sketches to McCutcheon]

MA: Do any of those drawings look familiar to you? MC:  Mm-hmm. Some of ‘em.

MA:  Like which one?



	MC:	I’d have to go back over ‘em again. Now let’s—don’t dwell on the same thing if you want to get as much information ‘cause I don’t have the time to really put into it. This is interfering with my business, see.

	MA:	I understand, sir.



	MC:	And my business keeping my wife going to Publix to buy grocery. So I’m not going to let anything else interfere with it too much.

	MA:	I’m going to be leaving in ten minutes sir, and I’m fine with that. MC:	See, what this says here? That burn from Gussie?





	MA:	Yeah. It says a burnt first floor, unusable. It says it was burned, so you couldn’t use the first floor and there were two wings on the side.

	MC:	They did use it. I don’t know nothing about it burnin; where that idea comes. See, she’s out in a nursing home now, and I guess that’s a thought thing in her mind. I don’t remember it being burnt. But it was a big floor! And it wasn’t divided. It was one big floor. It wasn’t divided with built-in partitions.

	MA:	Do you remember stoves? MC:	Wood stoves? Yeah.

	MA:	Do you remember where they were?



	MC:	Well they set ‘em in the—located in the best part of the room to get the most heat.

	MA:	And where would that be, sir?



	MC:	I’d have to be in the building to show you [Chuckling]. Yeah, but they were there. That was the only heat we had. When we got over to Dunbar we had steam then.

	MA:	You don’t remember an interior stairway?



	MC:	I don’t. There was one I think, but I can’t recall the location of where it was. MA:	And the stairs went straight up, it didn’t go along the side of the building?

	MC:	No, it couldn’t fit through the back. That was the exterior steps.



	MA:	Right, right. Do you have anything else to add, Mr. McCutcheon? I’ve got everything I need. Do you have any other memories that you can remember about the school, that you haven’t—?

	MC:	Even back in that time they had a fence around it. MA:	It had a fence?





	MC:	Because of the Anderson Avenue and Cranford Avenue traffic. MA:	Was it on all four sides?

	MC:	It was on Anderson and partially Cranford and nothing on Lemon, it was a residential section.

	MA:	Do you remember a gate to come into the school? MC:	Huh?

	MA:	A gate.



	MC:	A gate doing what?



	MA:	A gate, a swinging gate to get into the school. Was there a gate? MC:	It was just open, wasn’t no need at all.

	MA:	Okay, no gate. And the desks were not nailed desks?



	MC:	No, they were not fastened to the floor. They were moveable desks and seats.



The desk and the seat was made together.



	MA:	You had singles and doubles?



	MC:	Yeah, well, those were the singles. They made doubles according to the amount of students that would be there in that class where some may have to sit two together. I remember sitting two together at times.

	MA:	Do you remember it being crowded in the school?



MC:  No at that time we didn’t know what crowded was. As far as we were concerned at that time, the word crowded was not even in the English language, it was just getting together and making room for your friend or your fellow student.

MA:  That’s all I have, but do you have any more details that you can remember before we end this interview?





	MC:	We had a big playground out there— MA:	And where was the playground?

	MC:	And nothing to play on between the school and Anderson Avenue. That was the most open land and that’s where we played out there. We didn’t have playground equipment now, but we had room to play in. Land that boys and girls could make mud pies and grass dolls. The girls played with grass dolls. They’d get some— what’s it called that grass? [Ber]muda grass. Pull it out by the root and shape the thing out of it, the body part of the grass. [Chuckling] We played out there. We played baseball out there even after that school was closed because of the era.
