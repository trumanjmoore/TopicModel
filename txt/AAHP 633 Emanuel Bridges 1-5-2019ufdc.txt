	O:	I am Annie Otto and I am in the apartment of Rocky Bridges and we are here on Friday, the fifth day of January 2019. Rocky is a resident here at the Atrium and a friend of mine. All right, Rocky thank you for being willing to be interviewed.

Where were you born?



	B:	I was born in Newark, New Jersey in 1932, January 8, 1932. O:	Who was in your family then?

	B:	Was my father, Maurice Bridges, my mother, Dorothy Bridges and Maurice Bridges Jr. and my brother Malcolm.

	O:	Now what was your mother’s maiden name?



	B:	I didn’t know my mother’s maiden name. Maurice, his name was Maurice Benjamin Bridges.

	O:	And was there any other veteran in your family at the time you were born?



	B:	I don’t know my family that well because when I was born at the age of three, my mother abandoned me. And–

	O:	I’m sorry.



B:  I didn’t know who she was. All I know what they call her a Little Dot. And my great aunt found me, Little Dot and Maurice were both in their teens – didn’t know nothing about raising children.

O:   Right.



	B:	Maurice didn’t raise any of us. We never carried his name and we were told that Dot was our Godmother, I didn’t know she was my mother until I was seven years old. I didn’t know who Maurice was until I was thirteen years old. And our





stepmother made him come up to Montclair where I was living under my adopted name – and explained to her and explained to me and my brother why we were in foster homes. And why he and Dot were divorced and he remarried and she remarried because they were both too young to have children. That’s when I found out that Dot was my real mother and my real name which was Emmanuel Charles. I was named after my great uncle, Dorothy’s husband. They adopted me and I was baptized at the age of twelve in St. Paul Baptist Church in Montclair under Reverend Billups. And I went into submarine service right out of high school.

	O:	Where did you go to high school? B:	In Montclair High.

	O:	Did they have integration at that time?



	B:	We always were integrated. We never had no racial separation in the town of Montclair.

	O:	How about that.



	B:	You lived where you could afford to live and we lived in sections we called “Ward” – Third Ward, Fourth Ward. If you lived in the Third Ward, you were in a certain income bracket. Fourth Ward that bracket. That’s how we grew up.

	O:	How about that.



	B:	We never had segregation. O:	Wonderful.

	B:	Only thing we knew about segregation is what we saw in the news or heard on the radio. And we thought that was weird because we said well ain’t we all born





in America? And the only thing we knew about slavery is what we saw and was told – even the teachers. And then we start having Black history. And the teachers was starting to learn Black history and teach it. Because that’s all we knew. We never had no segregation.

	O:	You went to the Baptist Church?



	B:	Yes. I went to the Methodist Church first and then later to the Baptist Church after I was brought up and adopted by my great aunt.

	O:	It was a wonderful thing.



	B:	Yeah, she the one that found me and had me rushed to the hospital because I was dying of malnutrition. I’ll be eighty-seven years old on Tuesday.

	O:	You’re a survivor.



	B:	January 8th, I’ll be eighty-seven years old. This coming Tuesday. O:	That’s wonderful.

	B:	I’ll be eighty-seven.



	O:	We’ll have to have a party.



	B:	Well it’s on the board and they’ll probably announce it. O:	Mine was December 30th. So we’re close.

	B:	Right.



	O:	Well now, at the time that you graduated from high school. B:	Class of [19]51!

	O:	Class of [19]51. Was it a big class? B:	Yeah! Montclair, yeah.

	O:	Did you play sports?





	B:	Yeah I ran track. O:	Wow!

	B:	Because at our school you couldn’t play with Clary Anderson unless you had a minimum of 150 pounds then you could play football. If you was under 150 you couldn’t play. Play baseball or run track or something. ‘Cause Clary Anderson said “I don’t want nobody on the team under 150.” And our team was so bad, we were so bad that we were known as the Montclair Bulldogs. And the other schools they stopped playing us because they said we were physically too strong for them. And we learned that because when we were in junior high school our gym teacher was a former Marine and he put us through the physical training that they went through in boot camp.

	O:	So did he inspire you to go into the military?



	B:	Yes. Yeah because our third grade teacher, her brother was a bombardier and navigator on a B17. When World War II broke out we sold bombs and stamps and we bought stamps. And I said, “Well I want to go in that.” And we were so inspired by the Marines and said “We’ll go into the Marine Corp.”

	O:	Where did you go to boot camp?



	B:	I went to boot camp then they changed it from – we went to boot camp in Bainbridge, Maryland. Bainbridge, Maryland is where we went to boot camp.

	O:	At that time–



B:  We didn’t go to Great Lakes because they changed it. They changed the military status after Eleanor Roosevelt told Franklin Roosevelt “Desegregate the military” because I had two great uncles that were gassed in World War I and they fought





for this country, came back and couldn’t buy diners, couldn’t eat at a diner, couldn’t buy nothing. We said well “Why are we fighting for this country?” And when I saw it for the first time, I said “Ma weren’t we all born here?” And she explained to me about racism. I said, “Well I’m glad we don’t have it at home.”

	O:	So this was in Maryland? B:	No, Montclair!

	O:	Yes but where were you experiencing the racism?



	B:	Oh, well we went to Maryland to see my aunt. We went to get her to bring her up to Montclair. And we asked “Why did you want to live here?” She said well they just broke it down. They just broke it down.” Well when I met my wife, she had come from Amherst, Virginia and when she saw the way I was raised and the way I lived was new to her. She said, “Well we don’t have that where I come from. And we didn’t have no house like that.” She was born on a farm and it was seventeen of them, brothers and sisters all by one set of parents – set of parents. And her name was Johnson.

	O:	Life was hard



	B:		Yes and she said, “Your son never dishonored me.” When who I called my mother was my great Aunt, I was not raised by my mother, at all! She said, “I taught him to live as what’s life as in the Bible.”

	O:	Free person.



	B:	And said, “That’s the way he grew up and that’s the way he’s living.” O:	And you do!





	B:	And so she said, “Well, he never dishonored me” and he... talking it makes me choke.

	O:	I’m sorry.



	B:	And he wanted to meet my family and wanted my family to meet him. And I see y’all have this big house. We said, “We don’t own the house, we rent it.” [Coughs] And she said, “Everybody is like one, if you notice, we’re Black and the White lives here. It’s not all Black, we don’t have no such thing as an all Black neighborhood. She met my classmate and my neighbor, Angelo Murano and my father’s coworker, who was an Italian. And my father, my great uncle, they all lived together and worked together within the town of Montclair. And my father— and when I say my father I always talk about Charley because Maurice was a stranger to me. I didn’t even know who he was until I was thirteen when I met him for the first time and my mother said “You introduce yourself to him and explain to him why he doesn’t carry your name and when you see Malcolm, explain to him why his name is Hunter.” If the oldest boy lives out in the state of Washington and he’s different—

	O:	He went far away.



	B:	Well he went with a family out in the West Coast.



	O:	Now when you went from Montclair to Maryland, did that seem like a long trip?



	B:	Yeah because that was the first time we rode on the train that distance because we never left Montclair.

	O:	Was it scary?



	B:	No.





	O:	But when you got there—



	B:	And I went into the bathroom, that’s when I first saw segregation signs. That’s the first time I saw it and it was real weird because we never had any signs like that anywhere in New York or New Jersey where we came from. And I had said Ma, “Why do they have four bathrooms down here?” She said, “Four bathrooms?” I said yeah one says White men the other said colored Men – White women – colored women.” I said is that the custom here? She said “That’s racism.” I said “Well aren’t we all born here?” She said “These people that are here live under conditions.” That Black women and White women can’t use the same bathroom. Black men and white men can’t use the same bathroom. They can’t eat in the restaurants—

	O:	I’m sorry about that.



	B:	White people can go in a restaurant sit down and eat – Black people have to be waited on waiting at the back door and eat outside and take their food with them.

	O:	How did you handle that?



	B:	I said “Well this is weird.” And when a guy tried to pull that on me in the military and they found out I was getting ready to kill him. And when he saw, I said “Where I come from punks like you, we kill in the streets” but we don’t have that. My enemy is not in Korea, you’re my enemy and anybody like you is my enemy so I’m going to kill you. And show you, you’re not no master over me and I’m going to kill you. And when your mother and father step out the door before I kill them, I’m going to tell them. “I’m getting ready to go fight for your country, fight for our country”—I said “My two great uncles were gassed to come back here





after fighting for the country, fighting to save your life, you treat them like they weren’t humans. And when you make movies, you make movies like Tarzan that have a monkey, a gorilla with more common sense than the Natives.” All they do is just dance and sing, found out later that was a lie. When the Africans took power in Nairobi, Kenya they didn’t allow no Tarzan movies. Shirley Temple was bonded said “You can’t come back to this country yet, you have no respect for Black people.” And he said, the President, when they found out why I was going to kill this guy, they called his mother and father and said, “This is the United States Navy, not the confederate Navy and we’re kicking your son out and give him an undesirable discharge.”

	O:	That’s very good.



	B:	And said “If you want him alive when he—take him to the main gate, you better be out there to get him because he’s got six Black sailors out to kill him because he tried what you taught him,” he tried to pull it on them when they’re getting ready for combat. And we wanted to let you know this that’s not the Confederate Navy, the United States Navy. And the boy, he’ll get an honorable discharge if he killed your son because he’s going to fight for the country. They said, “Well we’re sorry, we apologize.” And the guy when he found out what it was really all about he came over and he said “I want to apologize.” I said “Now I’m going to show you what hatred is.” I said if “When I get back from overseas, I’m going to come exactly where you live and if you’re the same way, soon as you come to the door I’m going to kill you.” My father said, “Never wound your enemy, kill him. Let him know that he’s about to die. Defend what is yours.” He said, “That’s what you do.”





I said, “I’m going to do just that,” and when I came home and I asked my mother and father, I said “You’ll have to forgive me and my wife, see you’ll have to forgive me.” I said “Because the unit I was trained in, we were trained to kill.” And I said “I did exactly what you told me not to do.” We took no prisoners and we killed because the President said “Take no prisoners” and he knew in our outfit in in the submarines we didn’t take prisoners because we had nowhere to put them. I said and we did just that. And so she said “Well we understand that and you are forgiven because you had no choice and you are fighting to keep this country from going into a nuclear war and being destroyed.” And so she said “I understand that your daughter and your son are going to Florida,” and said “If you want you can go with them to Florida.” And this is how I down got here after my son was transferred down here and when he came out of flight school in Teterboro, where he became the first Black instructor, flight graduate from flight school and he went with me and joined the club that I was in. I was in a club where we had our own aircraft—four of us. And that would be four jets and we owned the aircraft at the flight school where my son, he taught flying and was inspired by it. And the airlines offered him a job. New Jersey State Police offered him a job because he used to work for the state police in Tulsa, Oklahoma.

	O:	What’s his name? B:	His name is David. O:	David Bridges?

	B:	Yeah, David yeah. David Lloyd Bridges.



	O:	Now, Cornelius is the father of your son’s wife?





	B:	Yes.



	O:	And he had a plane?



	B:	Cornelius didn’t have a plane he belonged to the fire squadron. O:	Well I did his history yesterday—

	B:	But I heard where they also made the mistake. He didn’t own the plane, he didn’t fly the plane. He took care of the engine the gunner—the guns on the plane—

	O:	I know that, but he told of how he learned to fly the plane and his wife learned to fly the plane.

	B:	Well he taught him but he never did fly—he never. And no one in his family flew a plane. My son flew the plane.

	O:	Well okay, all right.



	B:	Cornelius was attached to the fighter squadron— O:	Right did the guns and the bombs?

	B:	He made sure that the fighter planes, their guns worked and made sure that if they dropped torpedoes or bombs that they would work. That’s the only thing--

	O:	Yeah and be in the right place—



	B:	And they said “Well we’ll teach you about how to fly planes” and—



	O:	Well he told me that before he ever went into the military, he had a plane.



	B:	No he didn’t because in those days Blacks couldn’t fly planes much less drive cars! And he gets mixed up with what he do because his mind is ninety-seven. He never flew a plane in his life.

	O:	Okay.



	B:	Never! And he never owned one.





	O:	Okay.



	B:	Because in those days even during World War II they didn’t give Blacks guns to defend themselves.

	O:	Well you know when he was delivering cars? B:	Yes.

	O:	Well he told of how his wife followed him in a little plane and would fly him back. B:	Yeah.

	O:	He delivered the cars and then she’d fly him back.



	B:	Right, right. She would fly because she didn’t like riding that long distance and both ways.

	O:	Yeah.



	B:	But his wife never survived, she died.



	O:	Yes. Now when your son finished training to be a pilot how did he become an intercontinental pilot? Flying between Europe and here and around all the world?

	B:	Because after he turned—when the state police offered him a job—the major airlines, two companies said “No we want him to be our Chief Pilot because he came from the Black flying school and he knows about aircraft.” And so he said he accepted the job flying for the airline.

	O:	Isn’t that wonderful.



	B:	American Airlines and U.S. Airlines— O:	I know you’re very proud of him.

	B:	That’s how he started flying and they said “We want him to be our chief pilot and flight instructor.”





	O:	And he loves you very much—



	B:	Oh yeah, well [Inaudible 25:43] …have power of attorney—



	O:	Yeah, well now here’s what I want to do next, you married in that Baptist Church? B:	Yes.

	O:	In Montclair?



	B:	Yes.



	O:	You told me her name? B:	Ettie. Ettie Johnson.

	O:	Okay, she had a family of seventeen siblings? B:	Brothers and sisters.

	O:	Yeah, okay and where did she graduate from school or did she graduate? B:	No she never went beyond the third grade—

	O:	Bless her heart.



	B:	Because when they went to segregating you could only go as far as the school went.

	O:	How about that—



	B:	And she learned to read and write and her father learned to read and write and taught them. And then she learned from my great aunt—

	O:	It’s a big shock when she came to Montclair—



	B:	Oh yeah it was. When she saw that and saw my schoolmates and where we lived in the neighborhood, she said “I’m going back and tell my mother and father what you have here.” And her mother came up and said, “Wow!” She said, “I’m





going back and tell your father.” And she went back and the father said “When he comes down he’s automatically a member of our family, we accept him.

	O:	Wonderful—



	B:	And they said “We will give him— “they said if—my wife decided when she died she wanted to be buried in Amherst, Virginia where she was born, and she said “I would like for my husband to be buried with me.” And my family said “We accept that, we will do it.” And her family said “We accept that and we will do it.”

	O:	I see on your wall you have “faith” and “hope”— B:	Yes.

	O:	And I know you live by those— B:	Oh yes!

	O:	And I think she did too! B:	Yes! Yeah.

	O:	You had that in common.



	B:	Oh yeah well we did because we were raised the same way. And we had that in common.

	O:	Now when you left the camp, where did you go?



	B:	When I left boot camp I went straight to sea. First I was under destroyer escort then I went to submarine service. I volunteered and went to submarine service.

	O:	What was the destroyer like? Was life on the destroyer like?



	B:	Well I was a gunner and I was captain on a three engine and it was rough— O:	Where did you sleep?





	B:	We slept in the compartments where our guns were. A destroyer didn’t have but forward and after rows like in the submarines. When I was in the submarine, my bunk was over on top of a torpedo. And when we went into action my bunk would go like this—and the torpedo would go into the torpedo tube. And I was an engineman on the submarine.

	O:	And you lived through it!



	B:	And we lived through it, yes. We were the forerunners of the first nuclear submarine northwards. I was on the SS Piper 4-0-9.

	O:	SS Piper?



	B:	Yes, 4-0-9.



	O:	4-0-9.



	B:	Yeah I got a picture of it.



	O:	Where did you go? From where to where?



	B:	We went around the third Naval district area. And we also went down and went to Guantanamo Bay, Cuba.

	O:	Now tell me about that. That was at a time when who was President?



	B:	That was when Clinton was President. Clinton was the president, this is after Obama. Well before, yeah—

	O:	After George H.W. Bush? B:	After Bush, yes.

	O:	Then Clinton.



	B:	Yeah, then Clinton.



	O:	So it wasn’t Kennedy with the Bay of Pigs?





	B:	No, no, no. The Bay of Pigs. Because I was out of the military. In fact, we covered Kennedy’s funeral. Because I’m a retired producer and director. And we did music, Black history, religious music, jazz music, nightclub work. I went through all of that, not once did I drink. No drinks.

	O:	Nat King Cole.



	B:	Yeah, I knew him personally and I smoked no cigarettes. O:	Good for you.

	B:	Not even in the military.



	O:	That took a lot of self-discipline—



	B:	And I didn’t drink coffee, because the house I grew up they were from England and they drank a lot of tea. And the coffee they had, they wouldn’t cook it at home. I never had no store bought food in my life. Never ate no canned nothing because there were farms out where I lived. And later we found out they would call it—New Jersey is a farm state but a garden state because the Mafia people would bury their victims in New Jersey before they got caught. And so that’s why they would call it the garden state. And there was a farm near us and we ate vegetables that grew up on the farm. I never had no canned food at all—my mother and the only time we had any board ice cream was good humor. Board ice cream and good humor. Other than that the bread we ate if it was sandwiches, that’s the only time she would buy bread. And it would have to be bought bread that she bought for sandwiches only when we went on picnics.

And Malcolm X stopped me from eating bread, only wheat bread. And he showed me why. If you eat wheat bread or whole wheat bread it won’t turn to dough when





you eat it. He said take a slice of white bread, ball it up in your hands and when you do that it gets like dough – that’s what you putting in your stomach. From that day on I taught my wife, “Eat whole wheat and cornbread, don’t eat no white sandwiches.” So when we buy stuff like hotdogs or hamburgers, I said “Leave the bread, I don’t want the bread.” Give me the—

	O:	Well you’re very fit.



	B:	Yeah! I got reflexes at my age with one foot ‘cause I used to box, I was trained to box. And I learned that at the YMCA.

	O:	In Montclair?



	B:	In Montclair. Then when I went to boot camp, I start doing it at intercompany boxing. And I became so good that I beat the guys in the other area and I got the nickname of Rocky by my schoolmate because we were fans of Rocky Graziano. Rocky Marciano presented me with my trophy when I knocked out the Hawaiian Olympic Champion in [19]53 – I knocked him out in [19]52.

	O:	Wow!



	B:	When I had won my championship—had I knocked him out the year that I won my championship I would have been an Olympic champion as well. I took him out nineteen seconds of the first round. And after that they said, “You can forget it.” He said “They don’t want to fight you,” he said, “They said you too rough for them.” And one woman said “You knocked my husband out, you didn’t show him no mercy.” I said, “Your husband was out to beat me and I beat him to his punch. Your husband would tell you in our sport you don’t stand there for mercy.”

	B:	I had to learn to live like they lived in the submarine which was easy for me because I was in such condition, I had no claustrophobia and depth and pressure. I passed like that because of my physical condition. The fact that I never drank, I never smoked, never broke my body down.

	O:	And then did you have a special friend that you made on the submarine? B:	Yeah, Little Deer.

	O:	Little Deer?



	B:	Yeah because where I come from we have a place called Eagle Rock— O:	Yes.

	B:	And we were used to Indians and Whites and Blacks living together and we would picnic together—so we knew nothing about racism.

	O:	How about that.



	B:	And when Little Deer and I became shipmates, for instance, the guys would go to the movies. He tell me if they cowboy’s movies— “I ain’t gone let you see it.”

	O:	Yeah.



	B:	He said they are—prove to me that he is superior. “Go over me to get to the movie” and they couldn’t. He said “The only way you could get to the movie is if I say you can see it.”
