	D:	All right so if you could, for the record, state your name, when you were born and where you were born.

	B:	My name is Mary Elle Bryant. 5-18-[19]35. I was born in Gandy, you all came through Gandy.

	D:	Is that Elle as in E-L-L-E or the letter? B:	Right.

	D:	Okay. We can start with your grandparents, you remember your grandparents?



	B:	I don’t remember my grandparent but my uncle and my aunt and my grandmother raised me. Phillip Cotton and Viola Cotton, and my grandmother name was Erie Bouton. I don’t know my grandfather.

	D:	Do you know your grandmother’s parents by any chance? B:	Yeah, her name was Erie Bouton.

	D:	But do you know her parents, your grandmother’s parents? B:	No.

	KH:	Martha and Isaac Wynn.



	B:	That was their name. She went back and dug it up, she’s a digger. [Laughter] KH:	I like genealogy.

	D:	Do you know where they were from?



	KH:	They were from Campbellton, Florida up near Marianna, Jackson County. That’s where they were from. They were born there, but their family came from North Carolina. The slave owner, Benjamin Wynn, he bought land in Jackson County, and he transferred his slaves from Winton, North Carolina. Transferred them to





the land here, and he had farming, cotton, different type of produce, and horse training, they trained horses and raised horses, too. They brought that from North Carolina to Jackson County. That’s pretty much where the roots of my grandmother’s family come from.

	D:	What do you remember about your grandmother?



	B:	Oh my grandmother, she– my aunt got sick and my grandmother was living in Tampa. When she left Campbellton she moved to Tampa, her and her husband and four boys. ‘Cause my aunt and uncle had my mother, they took care of my mother, raised my mother. I remember my grandmother quite well because the day she died, I had spent the night with her—‘cause she was sick, she had cancer—and I said, “Grandmother, I’m going home and I’ll be back do you want me to bring you some peanuts?” and she said, “Yeah.” And while I was at my aunt and uncle’s house, Viola and Phillip Cotton’s house, I got some peanuts and I went back down to my grandmother’s house but when I got there she was dead. That was in, I think it probably was in the [19]60s I’m not positive. I think it was right in the [19]60s. I knew my grandmother quite well, but my aunt and uncle Viola and Phillip raised me.

	D:	And what did they do for a living?



	B:	Dad was a farmer, he was really a farmer. When he wasn’t farming he worked the Camp Blanding, but he was farming and working at Camp Blanding at the same time. And my grandmother, she worked different houses and she pulled moss just like Charlie did and they killed the moss and hung it up and everything. And Daddy was always planting something. He planted beans, peas, potatoes,





cane, okra, all of that. Dad would raise that. And he had different people help him gather it up.

	JH:	How many brothers and sisters did you have?



	B:	I was raised by myself. I got two sisters and one brother, but they, my mother had those for different men. They have a different dad than me, my dad only had one and that was me. But my two sisters and brother they had different dads.

	D:	I’m sorry, forgive my ignorance, but what exactly does pulling moss involve?



	B:	You pull the moss off the tree out there, and you put it down and you keep it watered and some people put lye on it to make it turn brown. You have to turn it brown, and then you hang it up on a line. In the meantime, it’s messy. You hang it up on the line and it dries, then you take it down and you always have a baler. A square baler. Put it in that baler with the wire and you bale it up and after you bale it up you take it out that trough they have there and then, Charlie had so much that he would carry loads of it out from Gainesville, near Ocala. We used to call him the “Moss Man” ‘cause he always had so much moss. But that’s what moss is.

	D:	What do they do with it?



	B:	In World War II, instead of having rubber like they got now in a chair, they used that moss and made filling.

	CB:	Upholstery?



	B:	Upholstery, yeah. And they also used it as bed cushioning. That’s what the moss was used for because down here you didn’t have cotton, but you had moss and





that moss took the place of cotton. So when later on after the war, they started using cotton and whatnot. But that’s what the moss was used for.

	CB:	They used the moss, they could buy that cheaper. As a matter of fact, it was cheaper and the rubber would last longer than the moss. The moss would get hard. If you’re upholstering cars they tell you never to use it for the upholstery.

	D:	So the cars had moss in the upholstery as well? KH:	Years ago they did.

	D:	I have so many questions.



	JH:	You said you grew up not far from here and you’ve been in Florahome in this area for your entire life?

	B:	No, I lived in Gainesville for some time. Out there in Grandin. I worked in Keystone, I was speaking to my husband about integration and I had another girl, Black woman, we were the only two Black nurses up there in Keystone. It was rough, it was really rough. The reason why we had it so good was because we took care the doctor’s office. And the Whites was all right, but they wasn’t where they are now. Because I could take care of the peoples in their office and go next door to the drugstore to get lunch and we wasn’t allowed to sit in there and eat our lunch. We had to take our lunches back to the doctor’s office and eat. Well, you go up the street a little further, and it was the same thing. They had a fountain and everything, buy your food, but we still wasn’t allowed to sit there and eat our food. We had to take our food back down to the office and eat. And they would get kind of bad one time because my boss, Dr. Christopher, his name was Donald Christopher, he didn’t like Black people, but he needed Black peoples to





run his office and for his patients. And I remember when they had NAACP, the first time I ever heard of it, Dr. Christopher was the one who told us about it. It was a Black couple that was gonna move into Keystone. Keystone is a regular White town. Now the Blacks are moving into Keystone but they still not welcome really. Dr. Chris came in that Wednesday morning and I noticed that there was something on his mind and I told this other girl, “Barbara, Chris ain’t looking right,” and she said, “No, he sure ain’t.” So we close at one o’clock on Wednesdays, and when we closed, he went back there and locked the door and we thought, “Lord what he fixing to do?” And then he came and he says, “Mary, you and Barbara, I’m going tell y’all something. It’s a family gonna move into Keystone and they Black. We don’t need a Black family here and the NAACP people are moving ‘em here. But if I ever hear of you all being a member of the NAACP you all can get your pocketbooks and leave ‘cause you’re fired.” We didn’t even know what he was talking about. We didn’t know ‘cause we were country people, you know. So when he left I told Barbara I said, “What he talking about?” She didn’t know she said, “I don’t know either.” But Black peoples had a hard way in Keystone. Actually, you couldn’t go to the bank and some of those grocery stores like Al Watson’s grocery store, he would always be following you around to see if you gonna steal something at the grocery store. And this one particular lady, she was working at a private home and she went in, and it was stupid of her because the lady had gave her a sandwich, and she was eating that sandwich as she went in that grocery store. He had her arrested because he said that she had stole the meat from there and made her a sandwich that she was





eating. Now common sense would’ve told you that you wasn’t gonna make no sandwich in the grocery store, but he had her arrested. So integration was really bad around here, it really was. Because Charlie said, when he was working up at the Lake Geneva, they didn’t even want him to go into the post office. And we only went into the post office because we was working for Dr. Chris, so it was kinda rough.

	CB:	They wouldn’t sell the postage stamp.



	B:	That was in Keystone, lots of Blacks have moved in there but I wouldn’t want to move in there myself. See I remember the times when they didn’t want us there, but it has really improved ‘cause Blacks got some of the most beautiful homes out there now you want to look at. And they could care less about those White people, they don’t care. Even schools are integrated. Not that many Blacks go to those schools. You might have a dozen, you might.

	KH:	When I was in high school, and I graduated in 2000, going to Keystone I can count on my two hands the amount of Black people that went to Keystone High School.

	B:	That’s right.



	KH:	On my two hands.



	B:	And that was just now.



	KH:	Yeah now it was about fourteen years ago. That was fourteen years ago. B:	It was rough.

	KH:	Even in my short thirty two years, I have seen a racial change in this area.



Particular areas: Keystone. When I was little, you just go up there to get your





chicken and eat it in your car, you going to Miller’s, even driving, you was a target. Driving into Keystone. The whole area of Keystone, Lake Geneva, going towards Starke, especially Starke. Starke is still heavy too now. It’s still heavy, you have to tiptoe through there.

	CB:	When the war broke out, that’s what civilized Starke them soldiers were up there, that’s what civilized Starke.

	KH:	It was rough.



	B:	It was rough, don’t let nobody tell you. It was rough, and it still can be rough.



Some of those rednecks now.



	CB:	You’d be surprised how many people that would hate you, laughing and talking in your face every day. You’d be surprised.

	B:	I don’t have no trouble going to Keystone.



	CB:	I remember a person to tell me, “I don’t like you.” They were laughing in my face and get behind my back.

	B:	I’m sure that’s the way they all look at me, because I’m friendly with everybody. It doesn’t make no difference what color you are, don’t make a doggone little difference. And even in my prayers at night and day I always say “God bless Florahome.” Because all kind of people live here, and I’m a child of God and I don’t have a color all people look the same to me. I’m sure that’s why I get along with ‘em, they don’t know which way to take me. You can be that way, they don’t know whether I’m friendly with them or I hate ‘em, they don’t know, ‘cause I treat ‘em all the same.





	KH:	And with Florahome you speaking from my perspective. Being a young African American female educated, they, Black and Whites around here would really treat me wrong if it wasn’t for them two. Out of all the African Americans who stay in Florahome and around this area, these are good people. So when they say, “Kenyatta, they say Charlie’s granddaughter, Mary’s granddaughter.” So that brings a level of respect and easiness from me if I go up there to the store. We know it’s a lot of rednecks around here but they treat and my mother different than what they would my cousins and them next door. Do that make sense?

	B:	Yes it does.



	KH:	It makes sense, so Florahome has its good and bad, but I’ve never experienced racism here in my lifetime ‘cause it was always “Charlie’s granddaughter” or “Mary’s granddaughter.” Even now with me getting my doctorate. If I was one of my cousins next door, some Black people around the corner they’ll get mad.

They’ll get upset.



	JD:	This is today?



	KH:	Today. But I can go up there and grandma and grandpa has told their friends like Mr. Picot, I could see him up there and he say, “Oh how you’re doing on your doctorate? Mary done tell me that you working good on it.” Stuff like that so me, being an African American female and standing behind them, they make it easier for me to live in this area. If I could pick Florahome up and take it anywhere around the world I would ‘cause it’s a lot of privacy, I have my loved ones here, a lot of land, like my granddaddy “tend to your own business.” That’s why I like





Florahome, but I’ve never really experienced racism here. In Interlachen I have, Keystone I have, Palatka most definitely.

	B:	Gainesville.



	KH:	Gainesville, too. Even going to UF I have experience it, but Florahome it’s like it’s a big family. It’s like grandma and granddaddy are a part of some of the White families around here, like the Parker’s, the Sturtevant’s. Like the main White families, landowners around here, grandma and grandpa are like uncle and aunt to ‘em, so that gives us a lot of leeway in it. It’s boring sometimes, to a young person, but as for living, it’s a cakewalk. You can go up there buy yourself a sub from Subway nobody bothering you, you can leave at night nobody bothers you. You really have to worry about nobody coming up in your yard too much, even at night. I like Florahome.

	B:	I don’t.



	KH:	They don’t, but I do.



	CB:	Yeah, [inaudible 19:38] some people are nice to me. I’m going put it like that. JD:	How did you get into nursing?

	B:	Into nursing?



	JD:	Yeah.



	B:	I finished school in 19[56]? KH:	19[54].

	B:	19[54], and this girl she was working up there at the time at the doctor’s office and she says, “Mary you want to work?” and I say, “Yeah.” So I started working up there and I left there and went to Palatka and worked at the hospital over





there and the lady, I looked on the chart and I didn’t see my name and I had been a nurse so I say, “I’m off all next week I see you don’t have my name up there,” and she said, “I tell you Mary, we gonna have to let you go because some of the patients say you wasn’t very nice to them.” And that hurt me to my heart.

So I went to two other doctors who I had worked with before, and I told them what happened and so Dr. Roy Campbell told me, “I’ll tell you what. I’m going give you a letter and you go to Alachua General.” You know about Alachua General, you ain’t been there. So I went over there, and I was hired the same day I went over there, but they didn’t have an opening in nursing so I went on into the cafeteria working, down in the kitchen. She told me, she said, “When I get an opening I’ll let you know.” She hadn’t got an opening, so I left from there and went to housekeeping and I worked there for a couple weeks and then she called me and told me that it was an opening in Labor and Delivery. Well, I went there and I worked there for almost two years. This lady told me they had an opening at Shand’s in psych, so I went and worked in psych for eleven years. I married over there in Gainesville. And I lived in Gainesville for quite a while and then I came back home and I married Charlie. That was it.

	JD:	So you say you finished school in [19]54, what— B:	Central Academy.

	JD:	Central Academy.



	B:	Yeah. I’ve been out of school sixty years. JD:	Wow.

	B:	This year is our sixtieth anniversary.

	B:	Yeah. My baby here she took me to one, and she gonna take me again in June. KH:	Um-hm.

	B:	Yeah she gonna take me again. JH:	To the sixtieth one?

	B:	To the sixty. Yeah she gonna take me to the sixtieth one. JH:	Y’all do a reunion every decade?

	KH:	Every year. JH:	Every year?

	B:	We do a reunion every other year. JH:	Every other year?

	KH:	Oh it’s every other year? I thought you guys had one last year too. B:	Every other year.

KH:  I’ve got to keep up because she’s got classmates leaving her. So I guess they got to have ‘em often. Need to have ‘em every year now. ‘Cause she just had one or two pass not too long ago. They were close, her classmates.

	B:	Kenyatta takes me mostly everywhere I go because her mother is not able to lift me, neither is Charlie, and when I have to go somewhere she can pick me up out this chair and throw me in that wheelchair like I’m a sack of potatoes. [Laughter] And she takes me mostly everywhere I go. She takes care of mostly all our business because we’re getting of an age now and there’s some things that we can’t do. We have it in our minds to do it, but it just don’t come to reality. So she





takes care of all of our business and stuff. I just handled the letters that she had to send for our—

	KH:	Absentee ballots.



	B:	Absentee ballots. So she’s good at taking care of me. If I can do it at night, she’s there, but forget the day. I can call at two o’clock in the night and she had jump in her car and come down here and see what has to be done. She just that good.

She take me to the doctor’s, she take her granddaddy, and take him wherever he got to go. She takes good care of us. We got other grandchildren, but we depend on Kenyatta mostly.

	KH:	I’m the only one here. I was living in Gainesville and I was working and working on my doctorate at the same time and it just got overwhelming, so I moved back home and my mom and grandma and granddaddy take care of all my bills that I have. So I say, well shucks, they call at two o’clock in the morning, I’m working all night studying, reading and writing anyway might as well come on down. I do what I can for ‘em, but like she said, during the day it’s a wrap ‘cause I’m asleep. That’s why I told you two o’clock, I’ll be up by two. [Laughter]

	JH:	That’s the prime time.



	KH:	That’s the best time for me.



B:   If my granddaughter gonna be taking to moving better take it now don’t take me in the day. But I can understand. She’s trying to get through, she’ll be through in June.

JD:  All right.

	B:	There’s a lot of things she would do for us but she just don’t have the time. So anything else you wanted to know about me?

	JD:	Yeah I wanted to know what it was like working at Shand’s at that time.



	B:	Loved it loved it loved it. Yes I loved it. OB is my first love, and psych is my second love. Yeah I loved it, but it’s done got too big. When I was there it was small and we didn’t go no further than the eighth floor, and now I understand they go further on up.

	JD:	What’d you like about it?



	B:	I loved the people. I’m a people person, and I love people. We get patients in, some stay two or three months and some– Now that was a problem, working at Shand’s and at psych, ‘cause those patients was allowed to go different places to eat, go shopping or whatnot, some of the patients was. And I had several occasions that when we all went out, they didn’t want to serve me. And I remember this one time that we went to the movies, and this man didn’t want me in there in the movie, next to the Silver Images downtown, actually the usher he came over and said—

	KH:	Where’s the Florida Theater now, the venue, yeah right there.



	B:	It was a couple of lawyers there, ‘cause they had had a nervous breakdown and whatnot, and one of them stood up and told this usher off and he say, “Let me see your manager.” He did and when they got through with the manager and everything—we never did go back down there no more—but he told him off. One time at Howard Restaurant, y’all know where the Howard Restaurant is? On Thirteenth Street going back to the mall. It’s Howard, what is it?





	KH:	They’ve been changing names on that.
